2.1 Out of Africa: The Original Expansion
The human story begins with expansion. Approximately seventy thousand years ago, a population of anatomically modern humans, perhaps numbering only a few thousand individuals, began moving out of the African continent that had been humanity’s home for hundreds of thousands of years [Klein 2009]. This migration, which would eventually place humans on every continent and in every habitable environment on Earth, represents the first great expansion and establishes patterns that have repeated throughout human history.
The migrants faced extraordinary challenges. They crossed deserts and mountain ranges, adapted to climates radically different from the African savanna where human physiology evolved, and encountered other hominin species who had preceded them into Eurasia. They had no maps, no knowledge of what lay ahead, no guarantee of survival. Yet they kept moving, generation after generation, until they had spread across the globe.
Why did they leave? The question has occupied anthropologists and archaeologists for decades. Climate change may have played a role, shifts in African environments that made migration more attractive or necessary. Population pressure in specific regions may have pushed groups outward. The development of new technologies (better tools, better techniques for obtaining food) may have made previously inaccessible environments viable.
But these explanations feel incomplete. Africa was not uninhabitable; many human populations remained and thrived. The migrants were not fleeing certain death but choosing uncertain life. Something beyond immediate necessity drove the expansion.
One compelling hypothesis centers on human cognitive architecture itself. The same capacities that make humans uniquely capable (imagination, planning, the ability to envision states of affairs that do not yet exist) may also make us uniquely restless. An animal that can imagine “somewhere else” will wonder what is there. A species that can plan for contingencies will seek environments where contingencies can be met. The cognitive revolution that made humans human may have simultaneously made us expansive.
This hypothesis finds support in the universality of the pattern. Every human population, in every environment, has expanded when given the opportunity. The Polynesian voyagers who colonized the Pacific, the Arctic peoples who spread across the polar regions, the Aboriginal Australians who reached their continent at least 50,000 years ago, all exhibited the same restless outward movement. Expansion appears not as historical accident but as species characteristic.
