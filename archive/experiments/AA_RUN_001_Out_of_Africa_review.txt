2.1 Out of Africa: The Original Expansion

Approximately seventy thousand years ago, a population of perhaps a few thousand anatomically modern humans began moving out of Africa [Klein 2009]. Humans had lived on that continent for hundreds of thousands of years. Over many generations the descendants of these migrants came to inhabit much of the rest of the world, and in their migrations we see a pattern that would recur throughout human history.

As populations moved into Eurasia they encountered other hominins who were already living there. They had to learn to obtain food in different climates, cross deserts and mountain ranges, and do much of this without knowing what resources they would find farther along their route. Much of what they needed to know they would learn as they went.

What prompted these migrations? A change in climate might have reduced the amount of food available to a population, and an increase in population might have had much the same effect on the amount available to each individual. With better tools and methods of obtaining food, humans may also have been able to live in places where they could not previously sustain themselves.

We know that other populations remained in Africa. This tells us very little about the conditions faced by the particular populations that left. But I think there is another question worth asking: would humans have continued to explore even where they had sufficient food and the means to sustain themselves?

We can imagine a place we have never been. We can also plan a journey to that place and make provisions for some of the difficulties we think we might encounter. Neither of these abilities explains why we would want to go there in the first place. Curiosity, coupled with these faculties, may have contributed to some of our early expansions.

Much later in human history the Polynesians settled islands across the Pacific. Other populations learned to live in the Arctic. The ancestors of Aboriginal Australians had reached Australia at least fifty thousand years ago. We cannot establish from these migrations alone how much of the impetus came from necessity and how much came from curiosity, but the recurrence of expansion in such different environments is consistent with the hypothesis that the disposition to explore is part of our nature.
